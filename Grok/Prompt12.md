**Recent developments in ethnobiology (2025–2026) emphasize ethical responsibility, epistemic justice, co-creation with Indigenous and local communities, future-oriented methods, and the careful integration of technology (especially AI) while addressing knowledge erosion, climate change, and biocultural conservation.** The field is actively reflecting on its future amid globalization, biodiversity loss, and digital transformation.

### Guiding Frameworks and Future Visions
The **Recife Charter** (published early 2026), emerging from the 5th International School of Ethnobiology held in Recife in November 2025, outlines pathways for a theoretically robust, socially relevant, and ethically responsible ethnobiology. It prioritizes epistemic justice, co-creation of knowledge, methodological rigor, expanded ethics (including non-human relations), social relevance, and responsible technology use.

Complementary perspectives include:
- Scenario forecasting for ethnobiology and ethnomedicine by 2035, exploring resilience of local ecological knowledge (LEK), AI-driven “in silico” applications, agroecology, and risks of further knowledge loss.
- Calls for a forward-looking ethnobiology that incorporates Futures Studies methods to co-envision and co-produce sustainable biocultural futures with communities.
- Reflections on European ethnobiology urging deeper commitments to an “ethics of care,” responsiveness to contemporary challenges, expanded contexts, methodological innovation, and greater societal impact.

### Institutional and Publishing Momentum
The *Journal of Ethnobiology and Ethnomedicine* marked its 20th anniversary in 2025 with special collections, a debates series on continuity and change, and the launch of the JEET Emerging Scientist Award recognizing early-career contributions. Ongoing collections address ethnomicrobiology, African ethnobiology (sovereignty and sustainability), food heritage, ethnoforestry, and island ethnobiology. The Society of Ethnobiology continues its annual conferences (2026 meeting at the University of the South, Sewanee, TN) and awards (e.g., 2026 Distinguished Ethnobiologist Dr. Robert A. Bye Jr.).

### Indigenous-Led Documentation, Conservation, and Threats
Fieldwork continues to document traditional knowledge, such as herbal practices and grassroots innovations among tribal communities in Eastern Nagaland, India. Spatial-phylogenetic analyses map biocultural diversity of medicinal plants among China’s ethnic minorities, identifying conservation hotspots. Studies highlight severe risks: climate change and language loss in the Amazon could erase a substantial portion of knowledge about nearly 5,800 useful plant species. Indigenous-led genomic and ethical research partnerships are also advancing.

### AI, Technology, and Ethical Challenges
AI applications are expanding for documenting oral traditions, monitoring biodiversity, predicting conservation outcomes, and supporting culturally sensitive tools (e.g., motif generation or knowledge preservation in Northeast India and Southwest Nigeria). Discussions stress participatory design, data sovereignty (e.g., CARE principles), and risks of extractivism or bias, advocating community-controlled, ethically grounded use.

**Overall trajectory**: Ethnobiology is moving beyond descriptive documentation toward a more applied, justice-oriented, and anticipatory discipline that co-creates solutions for ecological transitions, knowledge sovereignty, and biocultural resilience. Key challenges include knowledge erosion, equitable partnerships, and responsible technology integration. Central venues include the *Journal of Ethnobiology and Ethnomedicine*, Society of Ethnobiology activities, and related conservation and anthropology outlets.
