**Recent developments in ethnobotany (2025–2026) highlight Indigenous-led documentation, integration of traditional knowledge with modern analytical tools and AI, digital/social media influences on plant use, and stronger links to conservation, drug discovery, and biocultural heritage.** The field is shifting toward more participatory, ethical, and technology-enhanced approaches while addressing knowledge erosion and biodiversity loss.

### Indigenous-Led and Participatory Research
A notable milestone is the work of Hemerson Dantas dos Santos Pataxó HãHãHãi, described as the first published Indigenous ethnobotanist, who collaborated with elders of the Pataxó HãHãHãi people in Brazil to document 175 medicinal plants. Many uses (especially for diabetes, worms, and hypertension) aligned with existing scientific literature; the study emphasized community-driven recovery of knowledge amid habitat loss.

Similar efforts include extensive field documentation of traditional herbal practices and grassroots innovators among tribal communities in Eastern Nagaland, India (Tuensang, Noklak, and Shamator districts), focusing on local medicinal plants for common ailments. Language and cultural reclamation projects, such as those of the Tunica-Biloxi, integrate ethnobotany with bilingual guidebooks and habitat restoration for culturally significant plants.

### Bridging Traditional Knowledge and Scientific Validation
Researchers are applying advanced methods to ethnobotanical leads:
- A multi-institutional study analyzed a five-plant polyherbal formulation used by Konyak tribal healers in Northeast India, identifying compounds with anti-cancer potential (targeting VEGFR2) and lower toxicity profiles via molecular simulations and chemical analysis.
- The EthnoHERBS project systematically documented South-Eastern European herbal knowledge for skin disorders, characterized over 500 bioactive compounds (including 30 novel ones), and advanced sustainable extraction and cosmeceutical applications while supporting biodiversity conservation.
- A special issue of *HerbalGram* reexamined coca (*Erythroxylum coca*) as a culturally significant and potentially health-promoting botanical, covering its Andean/Amazonian roles, legal trade, and clinical research toward botanical drug candidates.

### Digital Tools, AI, and Methodological Innovation
Generative AI is being explored to digitize oral traditions, decode polyherbal formulas, predict herb-drug interactions, and accelerate natural-product discovery, with proposed ethical frameworks for inclusive use. Other advances include AI-powered apps for local plant identification and heritage preservation (e.g., AuroraLeaf in the Philippines), DNA metabarcoding/eDNA, participatory GIS/mobile tools, network ethnopharmacology, knowledge graphs, and high-throughput phytochemical screening.

Digitization of major collections (such as the ethnobotanical holdings at the Rio de Janeiro Botanical Garden) supports biocultural repositories and conservation policy. “Digital ethnobotany” is also emerging through social media, where plants like ghost pipe (*Monotropa uniflora*) gain new uses and prominence online, sometimes creating novel practices beyond historical records.

Quantitative and bibliometric approaches are increasing, with growth in statistical, machine-learning, and data-science methods applied to ethnobotanical datasets.

### Contemporary Practices, Conservation, and Conferences
Research on wild food plants explores the interplay between “traditional” and urban/peri-urban foraging, often influenced by digital platforms. Conservation-focused work links declining Indigenous knowledge (e.g., among the Punan in Borneo) with threats to specific medicinal species.

The Society for Ethnobotany held its 2025 annual meeting in Prague (first time in the Czech Republic) and planned a 2026 congress in Montpellier under the theme “Undisciplined Ethnobotany,” emphasizing decolonial, multispecies, and transdisciplinary perspectives.

**Overall trajectory**: Ethnobotany increasingly prioritizes community ownership, ethical benefit-sharing, technological augmentation for documentation and validation, and connections to global challenges such as health innovation, climate adaptation, and biodiversity loss. Challenges include knowledge transmission gaps, biopiracy risks, habitat degradation, and ensuring AI tools respect Indigenous governance. Progress appears in journals such as *Journal of Ethnobiology and Ethnomedicine*, *Economic Botany*, *Plants*, and *Pharmacological Research*, alongside regional documentation projects and Society for Ethnobotany activities.
