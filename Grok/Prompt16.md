**Recent developments in ethnozoology and wildlife-related traditional knowledge (2025–2026) center on systematic documentation of animal uses in traditional medicine and culture, the growing recognition of Indigenous knowledge for species rediscovery and conservation, legal innovations around animal rights, and stronger integration of local knowledge into wildlife health and management policy.** The field continues to highlight both the value of ethnozoological knowledge and the pressures (overexploitation, habitat loss, cultural change) that threaten it.

### Documentation of Medicinal and Cultural Uses
New ethnozoological surveys continue to record animal-based traditional medicine:
- In Andracha District, Ethiopia, researchers documented 52 medicinal animal species, with high consensus for respiratory and gastrointestinal treatments and notable use of cattle, bees, and goats. Knowledge varied by age, gender, and healer experience; threats included agricultural expansion and overexploitation, while sacred forests and community stewardship provided local conservation practices.
- In Jhargram, West Bengal (India), 57 animal species (birds prominent) were recorded for treating a wide range of ailments, including several novel uses and species. Quantitative indices (use value, fidelity level, informant consensus) underscored the ongoing importance of animal-based remedies.
- Parallel projects, such as an approved Indian Knowledge Systems initiative at Fakir Mohan University, aim to document tribal ethnozoological and ethno-veterinary practices in Odisha.

Studies of cultural demand for wildcat parts (skins, claws, teeth, bones) across much of Africa reveal widespread traditional use by leaders, healers, and in ceremonies—particularly of leopards and lions—highlighting an often-overlooked driver of hunting that conservation strategies must address.

### Indigenous Knowledge, Species Rediscovery, and Conservation
Indigenous knowledge has proven decisive in “rediscovering” species long considered extinct by science. In Indonesian Papua, local information guided the confirmation of two marsupials (including a new genus of glider and the pygmy long-fingered possum) that scientists thought had vanished thousands of years ago. Similar approaches contributed to rediscovering Attenborough’s echidna. Ethnoprimatological work with the Paiter-Suruí in the Brazilian Amazon documented detailed knowledge of ten primate species (several IUCN-listed), proposing certain primates as “cultural key species.”

Systematic reviews of tropical forest fauna research show increasing engagement with Indigenous and local knowledge, though full inclusion of communities in decision-making remains limited. Collaboration between pastoralists and wildlife health professionals in Kenya’s rangelands demonstrates how place-based knowledge of disease symptoms, transmission, and trends can strengthen under-resourced surveillance systems.

### Legal Recognition and Animal Culture
In a landmark step, two municipalities in the Peruvian Amazon granted native stingless bees legal rights to exist, thrive, and be represented in court—the first insects recognized as rights-bearing entities. This builds on national recognition of the bees as a species of national interest.

Broader conservation policy is also incorporating animal culture and social complexity. The Convention on Migratory Species and IUCN initiatives are advancing frameworks that treat culturally distinct populations (e.g., tool-using chimpanzees or acoustic clans of whales) as relevant units for protection, with calls for equitable engagement of Indigenous knowledge holders.

**Overall trajectory**: Ethnozoology is expanding from pure documentation toward applied collaboration in conservation, wildlife health, and rights-based approaches, while confronting challenges of knowledge loss, overharvest, and the need for ethical, reciprocal partnerships. Key outlets include the *Journal of Ethnobiology and Ethnomedicine*, *BMC Veterinary Research*, *Ambio*, and conservation platforms such as Mongabay.
