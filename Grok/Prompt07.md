**Recent work in medical anthropology (cultural anthropology of medicine) emphasizes entanglements of care, technology, environment, and politics amid ongoing crises.** The field is broadening beyond traditional studies of illness experiences or health systems to address planetary health, AI, post-pandemic afterlives, reproductive justice, and structural inequities, while becoming more collaborative and de-centered from Global North perspectives.

### Climate, Planetary Health, and More-than-Human Approaches
Climate-driven health disruptions, environmental toxicity, and ecological change are rising priorities. Journals such as *Medical Anthropology* are strengthening calls for work on environmental and climate-related issues. Ethnographies explore embodied ecologies of chemical exposures, energy extraction’s effects on chronic and mental health, counter-mapping of diabetes in the Caribbean (linking climate impacts on food systems), and more-than-human care in highland Asia or veterinaryized food systems. Multispecies and One Health/Planetary Health framings are increasingly integrated.

### AI, Technology, and Health Futures
Anthropologists are examining AI’s integration into clinical practice, diagnostic intuition, waiting times, professional roles (e.g., “medical tech facilitators”), and ethical decision-making. Studies use futures-thinking methods, collaborative tinkering between clinicians and algorithms, and critiques of AI’s impact on care temporalities or equity. Related work addresses digital twins, online abortion access, diabetes apps/devices, and long COVID community support (including multi-modal sensorial practices like choirs).

### Post-Pandemic Legacies and Critical Approaches
COVID-19 continues as an ambient structuring condition. Scholars call for renewed critical medical anthropology of the pandemic’s afterlife, including “narrative compression” that marginalizes long COVID suffering, stigma and rumor among young women with HIV during COVID in Kenya, embodied memory of Ebola preparedness, and pandemic memes/health narratives. Broader political-economic analyses track funding cuts (e.g., potential USAID impacts) and quantification processes.

### Care, Reproduction, Aging, and Structural Inequities
Key ethnographic themes include:
- Reproductive politics: IVF lab practices and embryo ontologies post-*Dobbs*, online abortion in Brazil, and related justice issues.
- Aging and end-of-life: dementia prevention expectations, solitary deaths in Japan, post-stroke rehabilitation inequities, and care crises.
- Chronicity and survival: cancer precarity for parent-survivors, pediatric heart surgery promises versus unequal access, and “fixing unfixable bodies” in elective surgery.
- Structural competence and Indigenous health: reflections from UN Permanent Forum on Indigenous Issues, decolonizing approaches, and shifts from cultural to structural/intersectional competence in related fields.

### Institutional and Methodological Shifts
The Medical Anthropology Europe (MAE) conference returned in 2025 (Vienna) under the theme “Redefinitions of Health and Well-being,” highlighting friction, interdisciplinary collaboration with health professionals, multi-perspective ethnography, and ethics of biotechnologies. Predictions for the field stress growing transdisciplinary work (with STS, clinicians, artists, communities), unavoidable themes of AI/technology, war/conflict, migration, and famine, plus gradual de-centering toward Global South scholarship. Journals (*Medical Anthropology*, *Medical Anthropology Quarterly*, *Medicine Anthropology Theory*) feature editorial transitions and ongoing ethnographic output on these topics. Educational and applied efforts include health professions education prizes, tabletop games for maternal/child health, and new programs bridging anthropology with health/society.

**Overall trajectory**: Medical anthropology is responsive to polycrisis conditions, prioritizing collaborative, ethically engaged, and multi-sited work that links intimate experiences of embodiment and care to larger political, technological, and ecological forces. It continues to critique power, inequity, and expertise while contributing practical insights to healthcare improvement and policy.
