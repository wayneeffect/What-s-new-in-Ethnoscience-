**Recent advances in ethnopharmacology (2025–2026) emphasize the integration of generative AI and computational tools with traditional knowledge, mechanistic validation of Indigenous formulations, reverse pharmacology and multi-target approaches, and responses to climate-driven threats to medicinal resources.** The field is shifting from descriptive documentation toward evidence-based, systems-level, and ethically grounded drug discovery and healthcare innovation.

### AI, Computational Methods, and Knowledge Digitization
Generative AI is being applied to digitize oral traditions, decode polyherbal formulations, predict herb–drug interactions, and accelerate natural-product discovery across Asian, African, and American knowledge systems. Ethical frameworks guide its use to ensure inclusivity and respect for source communities. Multi-agent LLM systems (e.g., TCM-Agent) automate network pharmacology analyses, literature validation, and visualization, reducing analysis time from hours to minutes while maintaining accuracy. AI-driven network pharmacology combined with multi-omics supports precision approaches, target prediction, and the design of synergistic formulations.

### Validation of Traditional Formulations and Green Approaches
Scientific analysis of tribal remedies continues to yield promising leads. A five-plant polyherbal formulation used by Konyak healers in Northeast India showed anti-cancer potential through compounds targeting VEGFR2, with activity comparable to some existing drugs and indications of lower toxicity. Green chemistry methods convert invasive plants such as *Mikania micrantha* into silver nanoparticles that catalyze drug intermediate synthesis and exhibit antibacterial and anticancer effects. Broader efforts document and validate medicinal plants used by Indigenous communities (e.g., in Madhya Pradesh and Nagaland), producing monographs and supporting reverse pharmacology pipelines.

### Systems Approaches, One Health, and Sustainability
Reviews highlight ethnopharmacology’s role in addressing metabolic diseases, antimicrobial resistance, and chronic conditions via multi-target natural compounds. Approaches such as reverse pharmacology, Ayurgenomics, and systems biology are advancing personalized and safer therapeutics. Medicinal plants are increasingly framed within a One Health perspective as dynamic biological, ecological, and socio-cultural components. Climate change is recognized as a major threat—reducing suitable habitats for many medicinal species and prompting calls for new research strategies, sustainable cultivation, and coordinated conservation. Projects such as EthnoHERBS exemplify the integration of traditional knowledge with advanced phytochemistry for both biodiversity protection and innovative products (e.g., cosmeceuticals).

### Broader Momentum
A major *Frontiers in Pharmacology* research topic (Reviews in Ethnopharmacology: 2025) brought together dozens of papers on bridging traditional knowledge with modern biomedical innovation, covering AI, systems pharmacology, and evidence-based validation. Databases of African natural products and expanded computational platforms are improving access to chemical and traditional-use data while emphasizing ethical bioprospecting under frameworks such as the Nagoya Protocol.

**Overall trajectory**: Ethnopharmacology is becoming more predictive, computationally intensive, and translationally oriented, while facing urgent needs for climate resilience, equitable benefit-sharing, and rigorous clinical validation. Leading venues include the *Journal of Ethnopharmacology*, *Frontiers in Pharmacology* (Ethnopharmacology section), *Pharmacological Research*, and related natural-product journals.
