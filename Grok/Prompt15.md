**Recent work in ethnobotany-focused forestry and ethnoforestry (2025–2026) emphasizes integrating Indigenous and local ecological knowledge with modern tools (remote sensing, AI, functional ecology) for forest health assessment, restoration, sustainable livelihoods, and biocultural conservation.** The field highlights community-led stewardship, bioeconomies that keep forests standing, and the urgent documentation of traditional knowledge under pressure from climate change, deforestation, and cultural shifts.

### Integrating Traditional Knowledge with Technology
Researchers in Papua have developed frameworks that combine Indigenous ethnoforestry classifications (e.g., old-growth forest, managed sago groves, regenerating gardens, degraded forest) with remote-sensing data such as NDVI time series. This produces more holistic assessments of forest health that also link to community well-being and support co-management. Similar efforts explore pairing Indigenous knowledge with AI for improved wildfire management, treating traditional practices as complementary to quantitative models rather than replacements.

### Indigenous Bioeconomies, Restoration, and Livelihoods
In the Brazilian Amazon, Indigenous seed-collection networks have generated significant income (over $1.7 million for nearly 1,000 families) by supplying native seeds for restoration projects, contributing to the planting of tens of millions of trees on thousands of hectares of degraded land—while keeping forests intact. Social forestry programs in Indonesia expand agroforestry (including coffee systems) to improve livelihoods, reduce clearing, and involve women more actively. Studies of Himalayan medicinal and aromatic plants and fungi underscore their major contribution to rural household income (around 30% in surveyed Nepali districts) and call for better community-centered management and sustainable trade.

### Documentation, Functional Ecology, and Conservation Priorities
Ongoing field documentation records medicinal and multipurpose forest plants among tribal communities (e.g., Eastern Nagaland, Sikkim Himalayas, Dayak Kenyah, and others). Spatial-phylogenetic analyses of medicinal plants used by China’s ethnic minorities identify conservation hotspots of both phylogenetic diversity and cultural importance. Research in western Amazonia links plant functional traits to specific uses (medicine, food, ritual), revealing patterns of “functional selection convergence” across Indigenous communities—plants are chosen non-randomly based on traits that reliably deliver ecosystem services.

Long-standing systems such as Gedeo agroforestry (UNESCO-recognized) and living-root bridges or sacred-grove practices continue to demonstrate effective steep-slope management, biodiversity support, and climate resilience grounded in traditional ecological knowledge.

### Challenges and Knowledge Loss
Declines in traditional agroforestry are accompanied by the erosion of specialized ecological lexicons (e.g., hundreds of terms at risk among the Manggarai of Flores, Indonesia). Climate impacts, market pressures, and generational shifts threaten both practices and the oral knowledge that sustains them. Ethnobotanists and Indigenous leaders stress that effective conservation and climate strategies require recognizing communities as central partners, supporting biocultural heritage, and creating economic incentives (such as bioeconomy models or payments for ecosystem services) that align with forest stewardship.

**Overall trajectory**: Ethnoforestry is moving toward hybrid methodologies that treat traditional knowledge and scientific tools as mutually informing, while prioritizing Indigenous-led bioeconomies, restoration, and governance. Key venues include ethnobiological journals, forestry and conservation outlets, and forums linked to climate and biodiversity policy (e.g., discussions around COP30). The emphasis remains on keeping forests standing through culturally grounded, livelihood-supporting approaches.
