**Ethnomicrobiology is an emerging interdisciplinary field examining the knowledge, practices, and relationships between human societies and microorganisms**, including traditional fermentation, soil management, health applications, and biocultural conservation. It has gained formal recognition and momentum in recent years.

### Formalization of the Field
A key 2024 review, “The dawn of ethnomicrobiology,” positioned it as a distinct area within ethnobiology. It surveys historical precedents and current work on human–microbe interactions in food systems, agriculture, health, and biodiversity. The authors argue for a shift from purely descriptive studies toward more theoretical and integrative approaches that emphasize co-evolution, symbiosis, and the invisible biocultural landscapes shaped by traditional communities. They stress the role of Indigenous and local knowledge in conserving microbial diversity and sustainable practices.

This has been followed by dedicated collections (e.g., in the *Journal of Ethnobiology and Ethnomedicine*) exploring multiple relationships between humans and microorganisms across cultural and ecological contexts, including food-medicines, agricultural systems, sensory and ontological dimensions, and the politics of knowledge.

### Traditional Fermented Foods and Microbial Diversity
Much recent empirical work focuses on culturally diverse fermented foods:
- Characterization of bacterial and fungal communities in traditional plant- and animal-based ferments from Nepal, South Korea, Ethiopia, Kazakhstan, and other regions, revealing interconnected microbial ecosystems influenced by ingredients, methods, and ecology.
- Studies of Northeast Indian Indigenous ferments highlight functional potential for gut microbiota modulation, nutrition, and pharmacology, alongside safety considerations.
- Research on Latin American Indigenous biotechnologies (e.g., masato, pozol, pulque) reassesses microbial fermentation, mucilages, and encapsulation methods for contemporary applications.
- African fermented dairy products and drought-resilient cereal/legume ferments are being analyzed for lactic acid bacteria diversity, starter culture potential, and metabolic adaptations.
- Educational initiatives integrate Inuit fermentation practices with microbiology literacy and Indigenous rights frameworks.

These studies often combine metagenomics, culturomics, and ethnography to document understudied non-European traditions and their health or sustainability value.

### Microbiome, Urbanization, and Specific Populations
Research links lifestyle transitions to microbiome change. For example, the SAMBAR project examined gut microbiomes across South Asian communities (including traditional Adivasi groups), showing how urbanization trajectories shape microbial composition and potential health associations, while expanding representation of these populations in global datasets.

Culturomics efforts have expanded known diversity in specific groups, such as Inuit oropharyngeal microbiota (bacteria and fungi), recovering previously uncultivated species.

### Broader Context and Outlook
The field intersects with microbiome engineering, natural product discovery from microbial communities, and conservation of endemic or locally adapted microbes. Challenges include bridging omics data with traditional ecological knowledge, ensuring ethical benefit-sharing, and addressing cultural erosion or industrialization of food systems.

**Overall trajectory**: Ethnomicrobiology is moving beyond documentation of fermentation or soil practices toward a more theoretical focus on human–microbe symbioses, biocultural diversity, and applications for sustainable food, health, and agriculture. It remains relatively young but is expanding through journals, special collections, and interdisciplinary collaborations that center local and Indigenous knowledge.
