**Recent developments in ethnomedicine (2025–2026) center on Indigenous-led documentation, scientific validation of traditional remedies (especially for cancer, diabetes, and infections), AI-assisted preservation and analysis, biocultural conservation, and stronger policy integration of traditional knowledge into formal health systems.** The field continues to bridge local healing practices with modern pharmacology, while emphasizing equity, sustainability, and knowledge sovereignty.

### Indigenous-Led Documentation and Knowledge Recovery
Participatory approaches are gaining prominence. In Brazil, Hemerson Dantas dos Santos Pataxó HãHãHãi—described as the first published Indigenous ethnobotanist—collaborated with community elders to document 175 medicinal plants of the Pataxó HãHãHãi people, with many uses (e.g., for diabetes, hypertension, and parasites) supported by existing scientific literature. Similar field documentation of herbal practices and grassroots innovators is underway among tribal communities in Eastern Nagaland, India, and validation studies in Madhya Pradesh have identified dozens of plants used by Adivasi communities for conditions including diabetes, wounds, arthritis, and skin disorders, with monographs prepared for many.

### Validation, Drug Discovery, and Green Approaches
Traditional formulations are being rigorously analyzed:
- A five-plant polyherbal preparation used by Konyak tribal healers in Northeast India showed anti-cancer potential via compounds targeting VEGFR2, with activity comparable to some existing drugs and indications of lower toxicity.
- Researchers converted the invasive plant *Mikania micrantha* into silver nanoparticles using green chemistry methods; these act as rapid catalysts for drug synthesis components and exhibit antibacterial and anticancer activity.
- Projects such as EthnoHERBS have characterized hundreds of bioactive compounds from South-Eastern European medicinal plants traditionally used for skin conditions, isolating novel metabolites and confirming antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and wound-healing effects while supporting sustainable cultivation.

Ethnopharmacology continues to accelerate reverse pharmacology, multi-target synergistic formulations, and approaches such as Ayurgenomics for personalized insights.

### AI, Digital Tools, and Knowledge Systems
Generative AI is being applied to digitize oral traditions, predict herb-drug interactions, accelerate natural-product discovery, and support ethical frameworks for ethnopharmacology across Asian, African, and American Indigenous knowledge systems. Mobile AI tools (e.g., image-recognition apps for local medicinal plants) aid heritage preservation and community use. National initiatives, such as expansions of India’s Traditional Knowledge Digital Library (TKDL), are linking traditional knowledge more closely with modern scientific and intellectual-property frameworks.

### Conservation, Policy, and Global Integration
Spatial-phylogenetic studies map medicinal plant diversity and use among China’s ethnic minorities, identifying conservation priorities. Medicinal plants are increasingly framed within One Health as dynamic biological, ecological, and socio-cultural components. Regulatory efforts are advancing (e.g., plans in Botswana to bring traditional medicine under formal oversight while protecting Indigenous knowledge). International cooperation is strengthening through the WHO Global Traditional Medicine Strategy 2025–2034, BRICS working groups on traditional medicine, and India–Africa partnerships focused on research, regulation, digital platforms, and evidence generation.

**Overall trajectory**: Ethnomedicine is shifting toward community-driven, technology-enhanced, and evidence-integrated models that prioritize biocultural conservation, equitable benefit-sharing, and practical contributions to drug discovery and primary healthcare. Challenges persist around knowledge erosion, habitat loss, intellectual property, and ensuring rigorous yet culturally appropriate validation. Key outlets include the *Journal of Ethnobiology and Ethnomedicine*, *Journal of Ethnopharmacology*, and related ethnopharmacology sections in broader pharmacology journals.
